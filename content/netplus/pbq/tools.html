Tools & Cable Faults

Pick the right tool for each job, then diagnose physical-layer faults from their symptoms.

PBQ 1 · Hardware tools

Pick the right tool

A technician has seven jobs today.

Your task

Drag the best tool onto each job. Two tools are not used.

Tools
Find which of 200 unlabelled cables in the wiring closet runs to desk 4B.
Confirm a batch of new patch cables has all four pairs in the right order with no opens or shorts.
Find a crack in a 3 m fiber patch lead by shining a red light down it and looking for the glow.
Locate a break 1.4 km along an outdoor single-mode fiber run.
Give an IDS a complete copy of an uplink's traffic, including errored frames, without changing the switch configuration.
See which channels neighbouring networks use around a slow conference room.
Check whether a switch port can transmit and receive with nothing else plugged into it.
Explanation
  • Toner probe: put the tone on the cable at the desk and listen for it in the closet.
  • Cable tester: its wiremap shows opens, shorts, and swapped or split pairs.
  • Visual fault locator: red laser light leaks out at a break or tight bend. It only reaches a few kilometres and only shows faults you can see, so it suits patch leads.
  • OTDR: sends pulses down the fiber and times the reflections, giving the distance to a break on long runs.
  • Network tap: a passive copy at the hardware level. A SPAN port needs switch configuration and can drop errored frames when busy.
  • Wi-Fi analyzer: shows nearby networks, their channels, and their signal strength.
  • Loopback plug: connects a port's transmit pins to its receive pins, so a working port brings its link up.
  • Unused: a crimper attaches connectors and tests nothing, and a speed tester measures throughput, not cabling.

Not in official objectives Objective 5.5 lists the toner, cable tester, taps, Wi-Fi analyzer, and visual fault locator as hardware tools. The OTDR, loopback plug, and crimper are not enumerated. They are kept because a cabling question can name the right tool for a fault without naming the fault, and these are the tools that do that work in practice.

PBQ 2 · Physical troubleshooting

Diagnose the cable faults

Six physical-layer tickets are waiting.

Your task

Drag the most likely cause onto each ticket. Two causes are not used.

Causes
A fiber link shows no light received at either end after re-patching. Swapping the two strands at one end brings it up.
A 10GBASE-LR module in one switch is cabled to a 10GBASE-SR module in the other. The link never comes up.
A 95 m horizontal run plus a 10 m patch cord at each end gives constant errors.
A new jack was punched down with the pairs untwisted 5 cm back and one pair on the wrong pins. It fails the cable tester.
On a 24-port switch, IP phones start rebooting once the 20th phone is plugged in.
An 80 m run of existing Cat 5e works at 1 Gbps but fails when the ports are upgraded to 10 Gbps.
Explanation
  • TX/RX transposed: each end is transmitting into the other end's transmitter. Swapping the strands puts transmit opposite receive.
  • Transceiver mismatch: LR is single-mode at 1310 nm and SR is multimode at 850 nm. Both ends must use the same type.
  • Attenuation: 95 + 10 + 10 = 115 m, beyond the 100 m limit for twisted pair, so the signal arrives too weak.
  • Improper termination: the fault is in how the jack was punched down. Untwisting causes crosstalk as a side effect, but the cause, and the fix, is re-terminating the jack.
  • PoE budget: the switch's total PoE power ran out, so it cuts power to ports.
  • Incorrect cable category: Cat 5e is rated for 1 Gbps. 10 Gbps over 80 m needs Cat 6a.
  • Unused: crosstalk and interference both cause errors, but nothing in these tickets points to them over the more specific causes.
