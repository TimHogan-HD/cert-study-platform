PBQ 1 · Wireless configuration

Configure the office access points

Three access points sit in a row along one office floor. Each one's coverage overlaps the AP next to it.

Your task
  1. Fill in the four settings on each AP.
  2. Fill in the authentication server that all three APs use.
Requirements
  • Employees can walk the length of the floor without reconnecting. The employee network name is CORP-STAFF.
  • Each employee signs in with their own directory credentials, which are checked by the RADIUS server at 10.1.1.20.
  • The APs use the strongest security they support. All three support WPA3.
  • The 2.4 GHz radios of neighbouring APs do not overlap, and the floor gets the largest possible number of non-overlapping channels.

Explanation
  • Same SSID everywhere: clients roam between APs that advertise the same SSID with the same security settings. A different name on one AP forces users to reconnect by hand. A real SSID is case-sensitive, so type it exactly. This lab accepts any capitalisation.
  • WPA3-Enterprise: "their own credentials, checked by a RADIUS server" means 802.1X, which is the Enterprise mode. Personal mode uses one shared passphrase. WPA3 is stronger than WPA2, and WEP and Open are never the answer when security is a requirement.
  • Channels 1, 6, and 11: in the 2.4 GHz band these are the only three channels that do not overlap (in North America). Each AP needs a different one. Any arrangement of the three is correct, because each AP overlaps only its neighbours.
  • 20 MHz: a 40 MHz channel in 2.4 GHz uses most of the band, leaving room for only one non-overlapping channel. Wider channels belong in 5 GHz and 6 GHz.
  • Port 1812 is RADIUS authentication. 1813 is RADIUS accounting, 49 is TACACS+, and 389 is LDAP.
PBQ 2 · Wireless troubleshooting

Diagnose the wireless complaints

The help desk has escalated six wireless tickets, and a new AP needs a channel.

Your task
  1. Drag the most likely cause onto each ticket. Two causes are not used.
  2. Read the Wi-Fi analyzer results and pick a channel for the new AP.
Causes
Step 1. Tickets
Handheld scanners drop off the network when staff walk to the far aisles of the warehouse. A survey there reads −84 dBm.
Laptops in the break room lose their 2.4 GHz connection whenever someone runs the microwave.
Users see a second network named CORP-STAFF with a strong signal near the parking lot. Its BSSID is not one of the company's APs.
Users must reconnect by hand every time they walk from the open office into the meeting rooms. AP-East was recently replaced and now advertises CORP-STAFF-2.
New Wi-Fi 6 laptops never connect faster than 54 Mbps on one AP, even standing next to it. The other APs give them several hundred Mbps.
Two APs at opposite ends of a hallway both use channel 6 at full power. Clients in the middle see constant retransmissions.

Explanation
  • −84 dBm is below the roughly −67 dBm that most designs aim for. The fix is more APs or better placement, so that is insufficient coverage.
  • Microwave ovens radiate at about 2.45 GHz. That is interference from a device that is not Wi-Fi. Moving clients to 5 GHz avoids it.
  • The same SSID from an unknown BSSID is an evil twin: a rogue AP impersonating the real network to capture logins. Enterprise authentication with server certificate validation defeats it.
  • A different SSID on one AP breaks seamless roaming. That is a roaming misconfiguration.
  • 54 Mbps is the 802.11a/g ceiling, so that AP is running a legacy mode.
  • Two APs on the same channel that can hear each other share the airtime and collide: channel overlap, also called co-channel interference. Change one channel or lower the transmit power.
  • The analyzer: channel 11 carries only a faint −86 dBm network. Channels 1 and 6 carry the company's own strong APs. Channels 3 and 9 overlap their neighbours and are never good choices.
