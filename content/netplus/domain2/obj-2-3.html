REMEMBER THIS
2.4 GHz = longer range, better wall penetration, but only 3 non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11) and more interference. 5 GHz = higher throughput and far more non-overlapping channels — 25 at 20 MHz in the US, of which only 9 avoid DFS — but shorter range. 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E) = cleanest spectrum, shortest range. Wireless Network Types

Four topologies are officially enumerated. The discriminator on the exam is almost always whether an access point is involved and how many endpoints the link serves.

TypeAP required?How it worksTypical use
Infrastructure Yes All clients associate to an AP, which relays every frame. Even two clients on the same AP talk through it, never directly. The default for virtually all business and home Wi-Fi.
Ad hoc (IBSS) No Clients talk directly peer-to-peer with no AP and no central coordination. Every device is an equal participant. Quick temporary file transfer, device-to-device links where no infrastructure exists.
Mesh Yes — multiple APs interconnect wirelessly and relay traffic for each other. Only some nodes need a wired uplink; the rest backhaul over the air, and traffic self-heals around a failed node. Large or hard-to-cable areas — warehouses, campuses, outdoor coverage, whole-home Wi-Fi.
Point to point Two endpoints A dedicated wireless link joining exactly two locations, usually with high-gain directional antennas aimed at each other. Building-to-building bridging where trenching fiber is impractical.
Distinguishing the four

Infrastructure vs ad hoc is the presence of an AP. Mesh vs infrastructure is whether the APs backhaul to each other wirelessly instead of each having its own wired uplink. Point to point vs everything else is that it serves two fixed sites rather than a population of roaming clients — a point-to-multipoint variant fans one hub out to several remote sites. Directional antenna selection for point-to-point links is covered in the antenna section below.

WPA3 uses SAE (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals) instead of the WPA2 PSK 4-way handshake. SAE prevents offline dictionary attacks — even if someone captures the handshake, they can't crack the password offline.

Authentication Methods — PSK vs Enterprise

WPA2 and WPA3 each ship in two modes. The encryption is identical; what differs is how a client proves it is allowed on the network.

PSK — PersonalEnterprise — 802.1X
CredentialOne passphrase shared by every user and devicePer-user credentials — username/password or a client certificate
InfrastructureNone — the key is configured on the APRequires a RADIUS server; the AP only relays the exchange
Revoking one userImpossible without changing the key on every deviceDisable that one account; nobody else is affected
Per-user policyNo — everyone gets identical accessYes — RADIUS can return a VLAN assignment or ACL per user
AttributionLogs show a device, not a personEvery session ties to a named identity
FitsHome and small officeBusiness networks of any real size
Why Enterprise solves what PSK cannot

A pre-shared key is a shared secret, and shared secrets leak. Once one employee's laptop is compromised or one departing staff member remembers the passphrase, the key is effectively public — and rotating it means touching every device on the network at once.

Enterprise mode also blunts the evil twin attack. With PSK there is nothing tying the network name to a legitimate operator, so a rogue AP broadcasting the same SSID looks identical to the real one. Under 802.1X the authentication is mutual: the client validates the RADIUS server's certificate before releasing its credentials, so a rogue AP with no valid certificate fails the exchange. That protection depends on clients being configured to actually validate the server certificate rather than blindly trusting it.

Channel Width

Channels can be bonded together into wider ones. Doubling the width roughly doubles throughput — and simultaneously halves the number of channels available and doubles the noise the radio takes in.

WidthAvailable inTrade-off
20 MHzAll bands — the baselineLowest throughput, highest channel count, most resilient in dense deployments.
40 MHz802.11n onward (5/6 GHz; technically possible in 2.4 GHz)Two bonded channels. In 5 GHz the US non-DFS budget drops from 9 usable channels to 4.
80 MHz802.11ac onward (5/6 GHz)Common default on modern APs. Non-DFS choices collapse to two.
160 MHz802.11ac/ax (5/6 GHz)High peak throughput, but in 5 GHz it is nearly impossible to use without DFS channels.
320 MHz802.11be / Wi-Fi 7 — 6 GHz onlyOnly the 6 GHz band has enough contiguous clean spectrum to support it.
REMEMBER THIS
Wide channels are impractical in 2.4 GHz. The entire band is about 72 MHz wide and already holds only three non-overlapping 20 MHz channels. A single 40 MHz channel consumes roughly two of the three, leaving no room for neighbouring APs and guaranteeing co-channel interference. Wide channels belong in 5 GHz and especially 6 GHz. The same logic applies in reverse in dense environments: when many APs must coexist, narrowing to 20 MHz gives more usable channels and often produces better real-world performance than a wider channel fighting its neighbours.

Regulatory Impacts — 802.11h, DFS and TPC

Wi-Fi shares the 5 GHz band with incumbent users — most importantly weather, military, and aviation radar. 802.11h is the amendment that makes this coexistence legal, and it defines two mechanisms:

MechanismWhat it does
DFS
Dynamic Frequency Selection		 The AP listens for radar before using a protected channel, and keeps listening while operating. On detecting a radar pattern it must vacate the channel immediately and stay off it for 30 minutes. Bringing up a DFS channel requires a Channel Availability Check — typically 60 seconds of silent listening before any client can associate.
TPC
Transmit Power Control		 The AP uses only as much transmit power as the link actually needs, rather than always running at maximum. This caps interference with incumbent users and, as a side effect, reduces cell overlap between your own APs.
Regulatory domains — why channel availability differs by country

There is no single global channel plan. Each country's regulator decides which frequencies are permitted, at what power, indoors or outdoors, and which require DFS. 2.4 GHz already shows this — the US allows channels 1–11 while much of Europe allows 1–13 — and the variation is far wider at 5 GHz.

Practically, this means an AP has a regulatory domain setting that must match its physical location. Set it wrong and the AP either refuses to use channels that are legal locally, or transmits on channels and power levels that are not — which is a compliance violation, not merely a misconfiguration. It also explains a support pattern worth recognising: a client device bought abroad may be unable to see an SSID on a channel its own regulatory profile does not permit.

DFS in practice
A DFS radar event looks like an outage to users: the AP changes channel with little warning and clients briefly drop. Intermittent, unexplained disconnects on 5 GHz that correlate with nearby airport or weather radar are a classic DFS symptom — and the usual fix is to pin the AP to the nine non-DFS channels, accepting fewer channels in exchange for stability.

Band Steering

Band steering is an AP feature that nudges dual-band-capable clients off the crowded 2.4 GHz band and onto 5 GHz. Left alone, many clients default to whichever signal is strongest — usually 2.4 GHz, because it travels further — and pile onto the band with only three non-overlapping channels while the far roomier 5 GHz radio sits idle.

The AP achieves this by selectively withholding responses: it delays or ignores the client's probe requests on 2.4 GHz while answering immediately on 5 GHz, so the client concludes 5 GHz is the better option and associates there. It is a nudge rather than a rule — a client that only supports 2.4 GHz, or one that has roamed to the edge of coverage where 5 GHz has faded, still connects on 2.4 GHz.

Cross-reference

Band steering also appears in Objective 5.4 as a remedy for wireless performance problems — specifically for the sticky client that clings to a distant or congested radio instead of roaming. This section is the definition; 5.4 covers applying it during troubleshooting.

Wireless Architecture Types

  • Autonomous AP — also called standalone or fat AP: Self-contained — holds its own SSID, security, and radio configuration on board. Simple deployment, no controller needed. Difficult to manage at scale, because every change must be repeated on every AP.
  • Lightweight AP — also called controller-based or thin AP: The AP is little more than a radio. All management, SSID config, RF optimisation, and roaming are handled by a central WLC. Best for enterprise. A lightweight AP cannot function without its controller.
  • Cloud-Managed: APs managed from cloud portal (e.g., Cisco Meraki, Ubiquiti UniFi). Zero-touch provisioning, centralized visibility.
Cross-reference

Autonomous and lightweight are the terms the official objectives use; fat/thin and standalone/controller-based are the same distinction under vendor naming, and the exam may use any of them. The full treatment of the WLC and the autonomous-vs-lightweight split — including why a lightweight AP is inoperable without its controller — is in Objective 1.2 under networking devices.

Guest Networks and Captive Portals

A guest network is a separate SSID for visitors and untrusted devices, mapped to its own VLAN with a route to the internet and no route to internal resources. Configuring one has three parts:

  • Separate SSID and VLAN — the guest SSID is bound to a dedicated VLAN whose gateway ACL permits internet-bound traffic only. Guests never share a broadcast domain with corporate devices.
  • Client isolation — sometimes called AP or peer-to-peer isolation. Prevents guest devices from reaching each other over the wireless link, so one compromised laptop cannot scan the phone next to it.
  • Bandwidth limits — per-client or per-SSID rate limiting keeps guest traffic from starving the business network.

A captive portal is the web page a guest is redirected to before being granted access — used to present terms of use, collect an email or room number, or accept a voucher code. Until the user completes it, the AP holds the client in a restricted state where DNS and HTTP are intercepted and everything else is blocked.

Cross-reference

This section covers the configuration side. Guest networks as a segmentation and zone decision — where the guest zone sits relative to trusted and screened subnets — belong to Objective 4.1. Troubleshooting a portal that fails to appear, most often because HTTPS enforcement or DNS-over-HTTPS defeats the redirect, is covered in Objective 5.4.

Key Wireless Terms

TermDefinition
SSIDService Set Identifier — the name of the wireless network. Can be broadcast (visible) or hidden (not broadcast in beacon frames, but still discoverable).
BSSIDBasic SSID — MAC address of a single AP. Identifies a specific radio/AP.
BSSBasic Service Set — single AP with associated clients.
ESSExtended Service Set — multiple APs sharing same SSID for seamless roaming.
ESSIDExtended SSID — the network name shared by every AP in an ESS. In practice ESSID and SSID name the same string, and the terms are used interchangeably; ESSID simply makes explicit that the name spans multiple APs rather than one. ⚠ Exam Traps
  • 802.11a AND 802.11ac are BOTH 5 GHz only. 802.11a is ancient (1999); 802.11ac is modern (2013).
  • 802.11n was the FIRST dual-band standard (2.4 + 5 GHz). Don't confuse with 802.11a (5G only, 1999).
  • 2.4 GHz has only 3 non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11). This causes interference issues in dense areas.
  • WEP is completely broken. Even WPA (TKIP) is weak. WPA2 (AES/CCMP) minimum for modern networks.
  • SAE is a WPA3 feature that prevents offline dictionary attacks against captured handshakes.
  • PSK vs Enterprise is about authentication, not encryption. WPA2-Personal and WPA2-Enterprise both use AES/CCMP. The difference is a shared passphrase versus per-user 802.1X credentials validated by a RADIUS server. If a question asks how to revoke one user's access or assign a VLAN per user, the answer is Enterprise.
  • DFS channel availability varies by regulatory domain. There is no universal 5 GHz channel plan — the AP's regulatory setting must match its country. A DFS radar detection forces an immediate channel change and a 30-minute lockout, which users experience as a random dropout.
  • Wider channels mean fewer of them. 40 MHz in 2.4 GHz consumes two of the three non-overlapping channels — effectively unusable. 320 MHz exists only in 6 GHz.
  • Band steering pushes dual-band clients to 5 GHz; it does not force them. A 2.4 GHz-only client is unaffected.
  • Ad hoc is the only one of the four network types with no AP. Mesh still uses APs — they simply backhaul to each other wirelessly instead of each needing a wired uplink.

Antenna Gain — dBi

dBi = decibels relative to an isotropic radiator (theoretical perfect omnidirectional antenna). Higher dBi means more focused radiation in a specific direction — not more transmit power. A high-gain directional antenna concentrates signal into a narrow beam, increasing range in that direction but reducing coverage area. Higher gain is not always better — it depends on the deployment scenario.

Wireless Site Surveys

A site survey determines optimal AP placement, channel assignment, and TX power settings.

  • Passive survey: Listen-only mode — the surveyor walks the area with a WiFi analyzer to measure existing signal, noise, and interference without connecting to any network.
  • Active survey: Connect to an AP and measure real-world throughput, latency, and packet loss at various locations.
  • Predictive survey: Software models RF propagation using floor plan data — no physical walk-through. Faster but less accurate; used for initial planning.
  • Output: A heat map showing RSSI coverage, dead zones, and channel overlap areas across the floor plan.
