Structured Cabling Components

ComponentDescription
MDF (Main Distribution Frame)Central point of a structured cabling system. Where ISP connection enters and connects to core switches.
IDF (Intermediate Distribution Frame)Connects floor-level access switches to the MDF via backbone cabling. One per floor or area.
Patch PanelPassive device that organizes and routes cable connections. Front = patch cords to switches; back = runs to wall jacks.
Fiber Distribution PanelThe fiber equivalent of a patch panel — terminates incoming fiber backbone runs and presents them as connectorized ports (LC, SC) for patching to switch optics. Houses the fusion splices or pigtails and protects the fiber's minimum bend radius. Typically where the MDF-to-IDF backbone lands.
Lockable Rack / CabinetAn enclosure with locking front and rear doors and side panels. A physical access control — anyone who can touch a switch can reset it, mirror a port, or attach a rogue device, so restricting physical access is a security measure, not merely tidiness.
66 BlockOlder punchdown block — used for analog voice (telephone). Not recommended for Cat 5e+ data.
110 BlockModern punchdown block — supports Cat 5e and above for data. Preferred for structured cabling.
Cable TraySupport structure that runs cable horizontally (horizontal cabling) in ceilings or under raised floors.
ConduitProtective tube for cables — EMI shielding, physical protection, and NEC compliance in exposed areas.
Lockable enclosures as a security control

Locking racks and cabinets matter most where the equipment is not already behind a controlled door. An MDF in a badge-access room is protected by the room; an IDF in a shared floor closet, a switch in a retail back office, or a rack in a leased colocation cage is not — and those are exactly the places a locking enclosure earns its keep. In multi-tenant facilities the cabinet lock is often the only boundary between your hardware and another tenant's staff.

This is one layer of a broader topic: physical security controls, including badge readers, mantraps, cameras, and asset tracking, are covered in Objective 4.1.

PoE Budget
Switches have a total PoE budget (e.g., 370W for a 24-port switch). If you add too many high-wattage devices, you exhaust the budget and ports stop powering. Always check the PSE (Power Sourcing Equipment) budget before deploying.

⚠ Exam Traps
  • Late collisions = duplex mismatch. If you see late collisions, check duplex settings on both sides of the link.
  • OTDR is for fiber. TDR is for copper. Don't mix them up.
  • 66 blocks are for voice/analog. 110 blocks support data (Cat 5e+).
  • 802.3af = 15.4W (older PoE). 802.3at = 30W (PoE+). 802.3bt = 90W (PoE++). Know all three.
  • PoE power budget must be calculated for the entire switch — not just per-port.

Power

Network equipment is only as available as the power feeding it. Objective 2.4 enumerates five power components, and the distinction the exam leans on hardest is how the three UPS types route the load.

UPS Types

All three deliver battery-backed power. What separates them is whether the load is normally fed from the mains or through the inverter — which determines how long the gap is when utility power fails.

Standby
also called offline
Mains → Transfer switch → Load
Normal Load runs straight off the mains. The inverter and battery sit idle.
On failure A transfer switch flips the load over to the inverter — a brief but real gap of a few milliseconds.
Fits Desktops and single workstations. Cheapest of the three.
Line-Interactive
inverter always wired
Mains → AVR + inverter → Load
Normal The inverter is permanently connected to the output, and automatic voltage regulation corrects sags and surges without touching the battery.
On failure Draws from the battery only on an actual outage. Transition is smoother than standby.
Fits Wiring closets and small server rooms — the usual choice for an IDF.
Online
double-conversion
Mains → Rectifier → DC bus + battery → Inverter → Load
Normal AC is converted to DC and back to AC continuously. The load is always fed from the inverter, never directly from the mains.
On failure Zero transfer time — nothing switches, the rectifier simply stops contributing and the battery carries the DC bus.
Fits Data centres and anything intolerant of a power gap. Most expensive; the constant conversion costs efficiency.
REMEMBER THIS
A UPS is a bridge, not a backup. Its runtime is measured in minutes — enough to ride out a brief sag, trigger a graceful shutdown of servers, or hold the load for the 10–30 seconds a generator needs to start and stabilise. It is not a solution for an extended outage. The exam pairing to remember: the UPS covers the gap until the generator takes over, and the generator then runs for as long as it has fuel. Naming terminology varies — "online" and "double-conversion" are the same thing, and "standby" is often written "offline."

Power Distribution and Capacity

ComponentWhat it doesKey detail
PDU
Power Distribution Unit		 Takes one high-capacity input feed and distributes it to many outlets across a rack. A basic PDU is essentially a rack-mount power strip. A managed (intelligent) PDU adds per-outlet current monitoring and remote switching, so a hung device can be power-cycled without anyone visiting the site, and actual draw can be measured rather than estimated.
Power load The total current every device in a rack draws, measured against the capacity of the circuit feeding it. Must be calculated before populating a rack, not after. Overloading trips the breaker and drops everything on that circuit at once. Plan to the 80% rule — a circuit carrying a continuous load should be loaded to no more than 80% of its rating, so a 20 A circuit is budgeted at 16 A. Remember that a redundant dual-feed rack must survive on one feed, so each side needs headroom for the whole load.
Voltage Common feeds are 120 V for general-purpose outlets and 208/240 V for higher-density racks. Higher voltage delivers the same wattage at lower current, which means less heat lost in the conductors, more equipment per circuit, and thinner cabling. Server power supplies are also typically a little more efficient at 208–240 V. This is why data centres standardise on 208 V while an office closet runs on 120 V.
Generator An engine-driven source that supplies the facility for extended outages. Takes seconds to start and stabilise, which is precisely the gap the UPS exists to cover. Runtime is limited by fuel on site and resupply, not by batteries. Requires periodic load testing — a generator that has never been tested under real load is an assumption, not a control.

Environmental Factors

Equipment rooms fail from their environment as often as from their wiring. Three factors are enumerated: humidity, temperature, and fire suppression.

Humidity

ConditionRisk
Too low — below roughly 20% RHElectrostatic discharge. Dry air lets static charge build on people and equipment instead of dissipating. A discharge that a technician cannot even feel is enough to damage a circuit board — and the damage is frequently latent, degrading a component that fails weeks later rather than immediately.
Too high — above roughly 60% RHCondensation and corrosion. Moisture forms on cool surfaces, corroding contacts and connector pins over time and risking short circuits.
TargetData centres are commonly held around 40–60% relative humidity — comfortably clear of the ESD floor and the condensation ceiling.
Exam trap — the direction matters

Low humidity causes electrostatic discharge, not high. The instinct to associate "moisture" with "electrical problems" points the wrong way here, and the exam tests exactly that reversal. If a scenario mentions dry air, winter heating, or a static shock felt on a door handle, the answer is ESD — and the remedy is to raise humidity, along with the usual antistatic wrist strap and mat.

Temperature

ASHRAE's recommended envelope for data centre equipment intake air is roughly 18–27 °C (64–81 °F), with wider allowable ranges defined per equipment class. The practical points:

  • Too hot — components throttle to protect themselves, lifespan shortens, and past a threshold devices perform an emergency thermal shutdown. An entire rack dropping offline with no configuration change and no power event is a classic cooling failure.
  • Too cold — mostly wasted money, but running below the dew point risks condensation, and very dry cold air brings the ESD problem above.
  • Airflow beats raw setpoint. A room can sit at the correct average temperature while a single rack cooks, which is why the hot/cold aisle arrangement and containment described earlier matter more than simply turning the thermostat down. Blanking panels in unused rack units stop hot exhaust from recirculating to the intakes.

Fire Suppression

SystemHow it worksWhere it fits
Clean agent
(FM-200, Novec 1230, inert gases)		 Floods the room with a gas that interrupts combustion by absorbing heat or breaking the reaction chain. Leaves no residue and does not conduct. The data centre standard. The only category that suppresses a fire without destroying the energised electronics it is protecting. Replaced Halon, which was phased out as ozone-depleting.
Wet pipe Pipes are permanently charged with water. Heat opens an individual sprinkler head and water flows immediately from that head only. Cheapest and most common in ordinary occupied buildings. Poor fit above equipment — a burst pipe or a single accidental head activation soaks the hardware.
Dry pipe Pipes hold pressurised air instead of water; water enters only after a head opens. Slight delay before discharge. Unheated or freeze-prone spaces — parking structures, warehouses, exterior risers — where standing water in the pipe would freeze and burst it.
Pre-action Requires two independent events: a detector must trip to charge the pipes, and then a sprinkler head must open before any water is released. Rooms where a water system is mandated but accidental discharge would be catastrophic. The double interlock is what makes it acceptable near equipment.
Deluge All heads are open with no individual fusible link. When triggered, the system discharges over the entire protected area at once. High-hazard areas with rapid fire spread — fuel handling, chemical storage, transformer rooms. Never appropriate for an equipment room.
⚠ Power & Environmental Exam Traps
  • Low humidity causes ESD, not high humidity. Below roughly 20% RH is the static risk; above roughly 60% brings condensation and corrosion. Do not reverse them.
  • Clean agent is the answer for rooms full of energised electronics. It leaves no residue. Any water-based system — wet, dry, pre-action, deluge — damages what it is protecting; pre-action merely makes accidental discharge much less likely.
  • A UPS bridges, it does not back up. Minutes of runtime for graceful shutdown or generator start. If a question describes an outage lasting hours, the answer involves a generator.
  • Online (double-conversion) is the only UPS type with zero transfer time, because the load never leaves the inverter. Standby has a real switching gap; line-interactive narrows it but does not eliminate it.
  • Deluge opens every head at once; pre-action needs two triggers. Dry pipe exists for freezing conditions, not for protecting electronics.
  • Higher voltage (208/240 V) means lower current for the same wattage — less conductor heat and more equipment per circuit.
